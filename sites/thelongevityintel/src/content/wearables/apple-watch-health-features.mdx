**The short answer:** Apple Watch is the consumer wearable with the most regulator-cleared notifications. On Series 12 and Ultra 4 that means an ECG app for atrial fibrillation (AFib), irregular rhythm alerts, sleep apnea notifications and hypertension notifications. Its VO2 max ("Cardio Fitness") number is a trend at best: two studies found it under-reads by about 6 mL/kg/min with roughly 13% error. Series 12 starts at $399 and Ultra 4 at $799 on Apple's site (checked 29 Sept 2026). It does not diagnose anything and does not replace a doctor.

## What the evidence says, feature by feature

- **AFib notification and ECG: the strongest.** The Apple Heart Study (419,297 participants, funded by Apple) found 0.52% got an irregular pulse notification. Of the 450 who returned a usable ECG patch, 34% had AFib on the patch, and 84% of notifications were concordant with AFib when the ECG was recorded at the same time ([NEJM 2019](https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa1901183)). Most people never get a notification, and a notification is a reason to see a doctor, not a diagnosis. Apple states the ECG app is for ages 22 and older.
- **Sleep apnea notifications.** The FDA cleared the feature on 13 Sept 2024 (510(k) K240929) as an over-the-counter device to assess risk. Apple says it is meant to detect signs of moderate to severe sleep apnea in people 18 and older who have not been diagnosed. Only a sleep test diagnoses it.
- **Hypertension notifications.** The FDA cleared it on 11 Sept 2025 (K250507). It does not measure blood pressure: it looks for patterns over 30 days. Apple says it is not for people under 22, people already diagnosed with hypertension, or pregnant people. A cuff reading is still the check.
- **VO2 max.** In a 2026 study of 40 healthy adults, Series 10 under-read by 6.25 mL/kg/min on average with 13.2% mean absolute percentage error, and it classified fitness percentile correctly for 14% ([Mayo Clinic Proceedings: Digital Health](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.mcpdig.2026.100357)). A 2025 study of 30 people found a similar 13.31% error ([PLOS One](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0323741)). The 2026 authors found poor individual-level agreement, though possible use for population-level research; the 2025 authors said the estimates need further refinement before clinical use. Use the direction of change, not the number.
- **Blood oxygen.** Apple's own footnote says the app is for wellness purposes only and not for medical use.

We found no independent head-to-head study we could cite for Apple Watch sleep staging or HRV against Oura or WHOOP, so we make no ranking claim. See [Oura vs WHOOP](/wearables/whoop-vs-oura-comparison) for what those two publish.

## Who it suits, who should skip it

**Pick Apple Watch if** you already use an iPhone and want cleared heart-rhythm and sleep apnea alerts plus notifications and fitness in one device. Choose Ultra 4 only if you need the 50-hour battery.

**Skip it if** you use Android (it needs an iPhone), you want a screenless sleep tracker (see the [Oura Gen 3 review](/wearables/oura-ring-gen3-review)), or you want a running-first watch with multi-day battery (see the [Garmin Fenix 7 review](/wearables/garmin-fenix-7-review)). An alert is not a diagnosis: talk to a doctor about any result that worries you. Compare the wider field in [best wearables for longevity](/best/best-wearables-longevity-2024).
