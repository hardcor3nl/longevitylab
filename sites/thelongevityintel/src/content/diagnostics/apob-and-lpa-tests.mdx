**The short answer:** ApoB and Lp(a) are two blood tests that look beyond the standard cholesterol panel. ApoB counts atherogenic particles; Lp(a) is a mostly inherited particle that independently raises heart attack and stroke risk. The 2026 ACC/AHA guideline recommends measuring Lp(a) at least once in every adult, according to the ACC's own summary, and gives ApoB a narrower role. On Quest's site (2 Oct 2026) Lp(a) alone is $45 plus a $6 fee, and a panel including ApoB is $215. This page describes what the tests measure and what guidelines say; it is not medical advice, and results should be discussed with a clinician.

## What each test measures

- **ApoB (apolipoprotein B)** is a protein on each atherogenic lipoprotein particle, so one ApoB roughly equals one particle. It is a particle count, where LDL-C is cholesterol mass. A 2026 comparison of the 2025 ESC/EAS and 2026 ACC/AHA guidelines says both frameworks share the premise that LDL-C and other ApoB-containing lipoproteins are causal drivers of atherosclerotic disease ([Eur J Prev Cardiol 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42579320/)).
- **Lp(a)** is an LDL-like particle with an extra protein attached. The [JAMA 2009 pooled analysis](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19622820/) of 126,634 people in 36 prospective studies found Lp(a) highly consistent within a person over several years and modestly associated with coronary heart disease (adjusted risk ratio 1.13 per standard deviation, 95% CI 1.09 to 1.18) and ischaemic stroke (1.10). The [2022 European Atherosclerosis Society consensus](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36036785/) calls the association causal and continuous.

## What guidelines say

| Document | Lp(a) | ApoB |
|---|---|---|
| [2026 ACC/AHA dyslipidemia guideline](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41824552/) (US) | Measure at least once in every adult's lifetime, as described by the [ACC](https://www.acc.org/Latest-in-Cardiology/Articles/2026/07/01/01/Prioritizing-Health), which says it is the first time the ACC/AHA guideline makes universal and genetic cascade screening recommendations; risk about 1.4-fold above 125 nmol/L and at least 2-fold above 250 nmol/L | Described by the ACC as useful in people with ASCVD or at high risk, and intensification can be considered for elevated ApoB even when LDL-C or non-HDL-C is at goal; "may be useful" in primary prevention. Class wording (2a for people on therapy, 2b otherwise) is from secondary summaries, not read at source |
| [2022 EAS consensus](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36036785/) (Europe) | "Testing Lp(a) concentration at least once in adults"; cascade testing in familial hypercholesterolaemia or with a family or personal history of very high Lp(a) or premature ASCVD | Not covered in the abstract |
| [2019 ESC/EAS guidelines](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31504418/) | Measurement "at least once in each adult person's lifetime" (Class IIa, level C) to find very high inherited levels above 180 mg/dL, as quoted in secondary summaries; full text not read | Not verified in what we read |
| [2025 ESC/EAS focused update](https://academic.oup.com/eurheartj/article/46/42/4359/8234482) (publisher page read 2 Oct 2026) | Measuring Lp(a) at least once in every adult's lifetime "should be considered" (Class IIa), and a new recommendation that Lp(a) above 50 mg/dL (105 nmol/L or more) is a risk modifier to refine risk estimation; the page's recommendation table lists level B for these | The page states no recommendation on ApoB measurement; it discusses ApoB-containing lipoproteins only as causes of atherosclerosis |

Differences to know: the European documents use mg/dL and nmol/L thresholds that are not interchangeable by one fixed factor (the 2025 ESC pairs 50 mg/dL with 105 nmol/L, while the ACC summary quotes thresholds in nmol/L), so check which unit your lab reports. We read the ACC's summary, the publisher page of the 2025 ESC/EAS update and PubMed records; the full texts of the 2026 ACC/AHA guideline (AHA journals, 403) and the 2019 ESC/EAS guideline (OUP PDF, 403) were behind publisher blocks, so those two rows rest on the ACC summary and secondary sources, as labelled. Do not read the table as personal advice.

## What to do with this

A test measures a number; it does not tell you what to do. Ask a clinician whether and when testing makes sense for you, particularly with a family history of early heart disease or high Lp(a).
