**The short answer:** two compounds have solid human evidence for a concrete benefit, [creatine](/supplements/best-creatine-supplements) for exercise performance and [vitamin D](/supplements/best-vitamin-d3-k2) for people who are deficient. Most trending "longevity" compounds sit at Emerging or Weak: small trials, blood markers instead of outcomes, or missed primary endpoints. No supplement here has been shown to extend human lifespan. Talk to your doctor before starting any of them.

## The ladder

Grades follow the [rubric](/database#rubric) (human evidence, effect size, safety, product transparency). A grade describes the strength of evidence for one named outcome; it is not a recommendation to buy. Monthly cost is the price of a brand's product at **the dose used in the cited trial** (not a suggested dose), from our [cost per study dose dataset](/cost-per-dose): brand pages read 28 Sept 2026, USD, before tax and shipping. ## What the ladder says

- **Correcting a deficiency is the best-supported use.** A blood test shows whether vitamin D or magnesium is worth thinking about; see [longevity biomarkers](/diagnostics/longevity-biomarkers-guide).
- **"It raises a biomarker" is not "it helps you live longer."** NMN trials measured NAD+ in blood; taurine and magnesium trials measured glucose, cholesterol and blood pressure, not disease.
- **Null results matter.** The vitamin D and standard omega-3 trials, the spermidine trial and the primary endpoints of the urolithin A trial did not show benefit. Roundups that leave this out are selling.
- **Ties to product makers are flagged** where the papers disclose them (urolithin A, spermidine, MK-7).
- **Prescription drugs are a separate topic.** [Metformin](/supplements/metformin-longevity-guide) and [rapamycin](/supplements/rapamycin-longevity-guide) need a physician; this page gives no guidance on either.
