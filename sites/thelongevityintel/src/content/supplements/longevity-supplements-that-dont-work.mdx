**The short answer:** for these popular supplements, the best human trials we read did not show the benefit the marketing implies. Antioxidant supplements did not lower mortality in a Cochrane review of 78 trials, and in the 56 lowest-risk-of-bias trials mortality was slightly higher (relative risk 1.04, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.07). Fish oil and vitamin D in the large VITAL trial, resveratrol, spermidine and NMN or NR also missed their main outcomes, and urolithin A was mixed. A trial that misses its main outcome did not show a benefit; that is not proof of harm, and it does not cover every group. Talk to your doctor before starting or stopping anything.

Grades follow the [rubric](/database#rubric) and describe how strong the evidence is that the supplement delivers the claimed benefit. "Weak" means a null or contradicted result in the best evidence.

## What this does not say

- **Not "proven useless".** Trials ask specific questions in specific groups; products named elsewhere on this site are discussed on their own pages, and every finding here is the cited trial's or review's. REDUCE-IT's prescription EPA helped high-risk patients on statins; CoQ10 has support in chronic heart failure, per our [CoQ10 page](/supplements/best-coq10-ubiquinol); creatine has the best support for muscle in older adults who lift, per our [creatine page](/supplements/best-creatine-supplements).
- **Not a reason to stop prescribed medicines.** Do not stop anything a clinician prescribed without asking them.
- **Deficiency is different.** A deficient person is a different case from a healthy adult; a blood test, not a supplement, tells you which you are.
