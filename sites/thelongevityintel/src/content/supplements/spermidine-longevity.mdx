**The short answer:** spermidine's best-known result is observational: in a 20-year Austrian cohort of 829 people, higher dietary spermidine went with lower mortality. That cannot show that spermidine caused it. The best supplement trial, SmartAge (100 older adults for 12 months), found no significant change in memory or secondary outcomes, and some authors disclosed equity in a spermidine company. Nothing shows a human lifespan benefit. Talk to your doctor before supplementing.

## What the evidence shows

Grades follow the [rubric](/database#rubric); overall grade: Weak.

| Question | Grade | Evidence | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Diet and mortality | Weak (observational) | [Kiechl 2018](https://doi.org/10.1093/ajcn/nqy102): 829 people aged 45 to 84, 341 deaths over 1995 to 2015; all-cause mortality per 1,000 person-years fell from 40.5 to 15.1 across thirds of intake; adjusted HR 0.76 per 1 SD (CI 0.67 to 0.86). Replicated in a second cohort (SAPHIR). | Food-frequency questionnaires; spermidine intake travels with other dietary habits. |
| Supplement and memory | Weak | [SmartAge](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2022.13875): 100 adults, 12 months, wheat-germ extract giving 0.9 mg/day: no significant change in mnemonic discrimination or secondary outcomes | Small; the authors suggest higher doses may be needed. Some authors disclosed equity and advisory roles at The Longevity Labs GmbH, and one was its chief executive during the trial |
| Lifespan in humans | Not tested | None found | The animal data are not human evidence |

## An earlier, positive pilot

Before SmartAge, a [pilot trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30388439/) (Wirth 2018, Cortex) randomised 30 cognitively intact people aged 60 to 80 with subjective cognitive decline to a spermidine-rich plant extract or placebo for three months. Mnemonic discrimination improved within the spermidine group with a medium effect size (Cohen's d 0.79, confidence interval 0.01 to 1.55, a range that only just excludes zero), but the between-group contrast the abstract also reports (d 0.77) had a confidence interval that touches zero (0 to 1.53 on the effect size; -0.01 to 0.35 on the raw difference). It was a phase IIa pilot, the authors called for a larger trial, and SmartAge, the larger trial, did not confirm it. The PubMed record we read carries no funding or conflict statement, so we cannot say who paid for it; SmartAge's authors disclosed equity and roles in a spermidine company (above).

Two cautions when comparing. Spermidine trihydrochloride is a salt: free spermidine is about 57% of its weight (molecular weights 145.2 and 254.6, our arithmetic), so 8 mg of salt is roughly 4.6 mg spermidine. And the amounts on the salt products are several times the 0.9 mg used in SmartAge, in a different form; no trial tested them. spermidineLIFE, the priciest here, is made by TLL The Longevity Labs GmbH (its product page), the company named in the SmartAge authors' disclosures, where one author was chief executive while the trial ran and three others held equity or advisory roles. The abstract does not say which brand supplied the trial extract, so we do not claim any product here is the trial product. Price is the smaller question: the benefit itself is unproven.
