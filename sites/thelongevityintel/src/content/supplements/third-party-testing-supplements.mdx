**The short answer:** the US Food and Drug Administration does not approve dietary supplements for safety or effectiveness before they are sold, and the manufacturer is responsible for safety and labelling ([FDA](https://www.fda.gov/food/dietary-supplements/information-consumers-using-dietary-supplements)). A named third-party certification is therefore one of the few outside checks on what is actually in a product. Look for a certifier by name: NSF Certified for Sport or USP Verified for general products, Informed Sport for banned-substance screening, IFOS for fish oil. "Third-party tested" with no name attached is a claim, not a certification. None of them show that a supplement works. Talk to your doctor before starting one.

## What each programme checks

Descriptions come from the programmes' own pages where our reader could open them (Informed Sport, NSF's sport site, IFOS) and, where it could not (nsf.org and usp.org returned 403), from search results, the sport regulator and a fish oil tracker, labelled below. We did not audit any certifier.

| Programme | Checks | Products | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| [NSF Certified for Sport](https://www.nsfsport.com/) | Finished-product testing for banned substances (NSF news items on its site cite 280+), and, per NSF's program description as returned by search results, label contents, contaminants, facility inspection and ongoing monitoring. NSF says it is the only independent certification recognised by USADA, MLB, NHL and the CFL | Sport supplements, functional foods, personal care (per nsfsport.com); certified lots searchable by lot number | [USADA](https://www.usada.org/substances/supplement-connect/reduce-risk-testing-positive-experiencing-adverse-health-effects/): reduces, does not eliminate, the chance of a positive test |
| [USP Verified](https://www.usp.org/verification-services/dietary-supplements-verification-program) | Identity, potency, purity (metals, pesticides, microbes), dissolution, manufacturing audit | Vitamins and minerals, some others | Short product list dominated by large brands, per a [fish oil tracker](https://verifiedsupplementdata.com/omega-3/third-party-tested/) |
| [Informed Sport](https://www.informed-sport.com/about/certification-process) | Every batch tested for more than 285 prohibited substances at an ISO/IEC 17025 lab (LGC); before certification, a paper-based review of the product and its manufacturing facility and pre-certification testing of at least three finished samples | Sport supplements | Screens for banned substances; it says nothing on whether an ingredient works. Run by LGC, a commercial testing company |
| [IFOS](https://www.ifosprogram.com/) | Fish oil (its site also lists krill and algae oil programmes): EPA and DHA against the label, oxidation, metals, PCBs and dioxins, published per lot (test list from a [tracker](https://verifiedsupplementdata.com/omega-3/third-party-tested/); IFOS's own site shows the lot-number search) | Omega-3 | A lot certificate covers that batch, not every batch of the brand; check the lot |
| "Third-party tested" (no certifier) | Whatever the brand chose to send a lab | Any | No named standard, scope or audit |

## How to check a claim yourself

- Find the certifier's name and search its public directory for the exact product and size.
- For fish oil, look for a lot-number certificate and match it to your bottle ([omega-3 page](/supplements/best-omega-3-fish-oil)).
- Treat a facility certification ("made in an NSF certified facility") as different from a certified product; our dataset records them separately.
- Sport certification matters mainly if you are drug-tested; for everyone else, the contaminant and label checks are the useful part.
