**The short answer:** taurine's anti-aging reputation comes from animal studies. A 2023 Science paper found that raising taurine increased health span in mice and monkeys and lifespan in mice. In people, a 2026 meta-analysis of 34 trials found lower fasting glucose, HbA1c and triglycerides, which are markers, not outcomes. Nothing shows taurine lengthens human life. Talk to your doctor before starting, particularly with diabetes or heart medicines.

## The premise was contested in 2025

The 2023 paper's starting point was that circulating taurine declines with age and that this deficiency may drive aging. Two 2025 studies contest that premise in people. A National Institute on Aging team, publishing in [Science](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40472098/), found circulating taurine increased or stayed unchanged with age in three human cohorts, and also in monkeys and mice, and reported considerable variability in how taurine related to motor function and energy handling; it concluded that a change in taurine is not a universal feature of aging. A separate [Aging Cell study](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41061678/) of 137 men aged 20 to 93, both inactive and active, found no association between blood taurine and age, muscle mass, strength, physical performance or mitochondrial function, and said this challenges taurine deficiency as a primary driver of aging in humans. Both measured blood levels; neither tested supplements or lifespan. They weaken the reason to supplement, not the animal result: the 2023 lifespan finding in mice stands as published, and whether raising taurine helps people who are not deficient is untested.

## What is known about safety

A [2008 risk assessment](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18325648/) of published human trials found no systematic pattern of adverse effects and called the evidence strong for the absence of harm at supplemental intakes up to 3 g a day in healthy adults (its "observed safe level"), adding that data above that were not enough for a confident long-term conclusion. Its authors are at the Council for Responsible Nutrition, a supplement industry group. It is a review of published trials in healthy adults, not a recommendation, and it says nothing about interactions with diabetes or heart medicines, which is why the doctor boundary above stands.
