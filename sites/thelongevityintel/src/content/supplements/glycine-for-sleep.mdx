**The short answer:** glycine, an amino acid, has a small set of trials suggesting people feel their sleep is better after taking it before bed. They are tiny and short. The best-described one is a crossover trial of 10 men enrolled and 7 analysed, run at the glycine maker Ajinomoto. The dose used in Bannai 2012 was 3 g before bedtime. No trial shows a health benefit beyond how rested people felt, so the evidence grade is Weak. This page quotes trial doses, not advice; talk to your doctor before starting a supplement.

## The trials, one by one

| Trial | Design | Dose used | Result | Caveats |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| [Bannai 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22529837/) | Randomised single-blind crossover; sleep restricted to 25% less than usual for three nights | 3 g or placebo, 30 min before bed | Significantly less fatigue and better psychomotor vigilance the next day on the authors' tests, only a tendency toward less sleepiness; no effect on plasma melatonin | 10 men enrolled (employees of Ajinomoto), 7 analysed (1 dropped out, 2 excluded for medication); authors at Ajinomoto, which makes glycine; the paper declares no conflict and says it was supported in part by a Japanese research-promotion programme ([full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3328957/)) |
| [Yamadera 2007](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1479-8425.2007.00262.x) | Volunteers with sleep complaints, with polysomnography, per Bannai 2012 | 3 g before bedtime, per Bannai 2012 | Bannai 2012 describes it as showing a shorter sleep latency on polysomnography | Abstract and sample size not available to us; one author, Bannai, is an Ajinomoto researcher in the 2012 paper, other affiliations not checked |
| [Inagawa 2006](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1479-8425.2006.00193.x) | Subjective sleep-quality trial in people with sleep complaints, per Bannai 2012 | 3 g before bedtime, per Bannai 2012 | Bannai 2012 describes it as showing better subjective sleep quality | Abstract not available to us at source |
| [GeroScience review 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37851316/) | Systematic review of glycine in adults (18 studies in healthy people, 34 in patients) | Various | The review says longer-term glycine improved sleep in healthy populations, but "these studies had small sample sizes with a high risk of bias" | Glycine was given for up to 14 days in healthy people and up to 4 months in patients; the abstract reports no conflict of interest |
| [Kawai 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25533534/) | Rodent mechanism study | n/a | Glycine's sleep-promoting and body-temperature-lowering effect depends on NMDA receptors in the suprachiasmatic nucleus | Animal; not evidence of human benefit |

Reading across: the human evidence is a handful of small trials from one research group, measuring how people felt and short-term sleep measures. Objective data (polysomnography) exist in one trial, as described by Bannai 2012. None followed people for more than a few days, and we found no large independent replication. A [2012 review by the same group](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22293292/) proposes a mechanism, lower core body temperature at sleep onset, that rests largely on rat data.
